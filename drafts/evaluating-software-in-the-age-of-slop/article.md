# Evaluating Software in the Age of Slop

Somewhere in the last couple of years, without anyone announcing it, the most useful heuristic in open source quietly died: the assumption that a project that looks finished probably is.

Stars, a polished README, a long feature list, a green CI badge — none of these were ever proof. But they used to be evidence, because faking them cost nearly as much as being real. Nobody wrote docs for twenty features that didn't exist. AI made the polish free without making the product free. The correlation between "looks done" and "is done" didn't erode.

It broke. I know because I paid for the lesson twice.

The first time was a tool called cass, which indexes every AI coding session on your machine — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, twenty-odd others — into one searchable database. Sub-60ms search. Optional semantic search, running locally. A slick three-pane TUI. Cross-machine indexing over SSH. A thousand stars. I had already built my own little tool for exactly this — searching my past Claude Code sessions — so I knew the problem was real, and cass read like my tool taken seriously: same idea, executed by someone who'd gone ten times further. My reaction was not measured interest — it was "holy shit, holy grail, let's use it." Which, to be fair to myself, is exactly what a good pitch is supposed to do to you. The better the pitch, the less I checked.

It broke in the first five minutes. And here's the part worth studying: that didn't stop me. I looked at the first bug and thought "small bug, okay" and started fixing it. The fix led straight into more broken code. That code into more. It ended up broken in four different places, and I was several hours deep before I admitted what I was holding. I hadn't found a bug. I'd taken a core sample.

The second time stung more, because the second time I had a tool for exactly this: an automated due-diligence pass I run against a repo before adopting it. Test coverage, maintenance health, bus factor, release cadence — the standard checklist, mechanized. The candidate was claude-code-telegram, a bridge that lets you drive Claude Code from your phone through Telegram. Here too I had my own scrappy version already running — and claude-code-telegram sounded like my tool with the issues already worked out. Not a holy grail this time, just the sensible-sounding upgrade: same idea, more features, someone else maintaining it.

This time I started with the cheap check: I pointed it at one of my own bots and ran it for real. It technically started — and then the first thing the agent tried to do was ask me a question, and the machinery for delivering that question to me had simply never been built. Not a rough edge on a working tool — a load-bearing piece that didn't exist. Only then did I run my evaluator over the repo, and its verdict, once you read past the hedging, was "don't use this." Correct! Also not quite the point. It couldn't say the one sentence that actually described the situation — "this doesn't work as written" — because it never runs anything; it reads the proxies and infers. I asked Claude to scope what it would take to get the thing into real shape, and the answer was: a lot. I gave up before finding out what else was broken. Less "complete failure," more "sounded like the finished version of my tool, was actually a pile of tech debt with a good README."

Two different tools, two different burns, one disease. Both times, the pitch that got past my defenses was the same pitch: *your tool, but grown up.* And both times, every signal I could read without running the thing said "real project."

Here's the thing — reading those signals used to work. Not because gut instinct is good methodology, but because of what the signals cost. Nobody wrote detailed docs for twenty features that didn't exist. Nobody built a feature matrix, a slick TUI, a wall of examples, without a working product underneath, because the surface *was* most of the work. A finished-looking repo meant someone had spent months in there. Going with your gut on a well-fleshed-out project wasn't diligence-skipping — it was a rational bet on the economics of effort.

AI didn't break your gut. It broke the economics your gut was trained on. The surface is now free — a gorgeous README, a long feature list, a green badge, generated in an afternoon — while a product that actually works is still expensive. The signal didn't get noisier. The thing it signaled stopped existing.

The obvious moral is the one half of you is already yelling at me: *you have to actually try the tool, dumbass.* Fair. Except I did try it. Both times. The five-minute check worked both times — cass broke within five minutes, claude-code-telegram broke within five minutes. Detection was never the problem.

The problem was what I did with the detection. When cass hit its first bug, I thought "small bug, okay" — and in the world my instincts were trained in, that was the correct read. A project with that much built-out surface had months of work in it, so a bug was probably just a bug: an isolated defect in something fundamentally sound. That's what a crack in an expensive surface used to mean. But cass's surface wasn't expensive. Its first crack wasn't an outlier — it was a fair sample of everything I hadn't looked at yet.

Trying it produces evidence. You still have to price the evidence, and every instinct you have for pricing it was calibrated in a market that no longer exists.

Even the diligence I did do fed on the surface. Before committing to cass, I asked an AI for an honest review of it against my own tool. The verdict came back: "better by a wide margin." And it wasn't lying — everything it could see supported that. Everything it could see was the part that's now free to fake.

The same goes for my automated evaluator. Its checks — test coverage, maintenance health, release cadence — are measurements of the *trappings* of a healthy project. The trappings used to be expensive; that's the only reason measuring them ever worked. The whole standard due-diligence toolkit — the mechanized one I built, and the informal one in your head — is an appraiser working from price sheets printed before the crash.

To be fair to the old signals — and to explain why my evaluator still got claude-code-telegram roughly right — they still work in one direction. A repo with no tests, no docs, and a dead commit log is still exactly what it looks like. The absence of polish still damns. It's the presence of polish that stopped meaning anything. The old signals can still say "don't." What they can't say anymore is "yes" — and "yes" is the answer you actually need when you're about to sink a week into someone's tool.

So what's actually left? One signal. The only one whose price didn't collapse: the thing working, on your problem, on your machine, right now. A README can be generated in an afternoon. A feature list, a badge, a wall of examples — free, free, free. Working software on your real use case is the one thing nobody can fake, because it isn't a signal of the product — it *is* the product. The five-minute check didn't get promoted because it's a great test. It got promoted because it's the last test whose result can't be typeset.

And even it comes with no guarantee. I got lucky with cass — the cracks showed in the first five minutes. If the broken parts had been two layers deeper, I'd have adopted it, built on top of it, and found out weeks in, with my own work stacked on the rubble. A clean first five minutes doesn't mean the quality goes deep. It means you haven't hit bottom yet.

Which is why the real change isn't a new checklist — it's a new default. With claude-code-telegram, the moment I hit friction, my first thought wasn't "let me debug this." It was "oh no, is this another one?" That reflex didn't exist before cass, and I've stopped thinking of it as damage. The tool didn't change between my two evaluations — I did. And that flip, from default-trust to default-suspicion, is just the correct calibration for the new prices. Default-trust was rational when polish was expensive. Polish is free now. So: a repo is a claim, not a product, until it has run on your problem, in your hands — and even then, it's on probation.
